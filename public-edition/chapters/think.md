# Do enough of the thinking to direct the work.
> A rough first attempt gives you something to compare with AI's response. You need enough of your own understanding to recognize a useful suggestion, challenge an assumption, and decide when the work has improved.
@nav Think before you ask

## Choose a first pass that fits the task.

You do not need to finish a brief before asking AI to help with a heading. But you should understand the point that heading needs to make. Otherwise, a more forceful version can quietly change the argument, and you may not notice what you have traded away.

The same principle applies at different stages of the work. Before seeking help with an outline, identify what the reader needs explained and which points matter most. Before asking for a critique of an argument, write down the argument and its strongest support. For a sentence edit, try to describe the problem: perhaps the sentence hides who acted, or asks the reader to hold an exception in mind for too long.

Your first pass does not have to be impressive. It needs to be useful to you. It establishes what you think before another answer enters the conversation and gives you a more specific way to ask for help.

## Assess your own work before reading the feedback.

Here is an exercise I use to make that first pass concrete. Take four minutes to explain your current answer, where your reasoning is strongest, and where it is weakest. Identify what you still need to check and what feedback would help most.

Then seek feedback from a permitted AI tool or another person. Compare an important suggestion with your original assessment. Did it reveal something you missed? Did it confirm a concern you already had? Or did it sound persuasive without addressing your actual problem?

The worksheet keeps your initial assessment separate from your later reflection. Four minutes is a suggested pause, not a scientifically established threshold for legal work. The point is to give your own assessment a chance to develop before someone else's version arrives.

## Notice how a choice of priorities changes the assignment.

In the employee-departure exercise below, a sales director's credentials were used to export company information shortly before she resigned. There are also facts about shared access, customer-initiated contact, the timing of suit, and the breadth of the relief the company seeks. Where would you begin?

I have used this hypothetical with lawyers and judges, and their priorities differ. That disagreement is useful. A lawyer focused on attribution may investigate the shared credentials first. Another may begin with the delay before filing or with whether the requested order goes beyond the company's evidence. Each choice directs the work that follows.

Read the facts and choose your priorities before opening the discussion. The exercise supplies no controlling law, so it cannot establish who should win. It asks you to explain what deserves investigation and what information could change your view. Compare that reasoning with the prepared discussion rather than looking for a single preferred ranking.

## Return to the problem after considering the suggestions.

Try a second pass after AI has expanded or challenged your initial view. Set its response aside for a moment and reconsider the client's question. What deserves more attention now? What has disappeared from the analysis? What alternative can you develop that the response did not offer?

In workshops, I ask participants to add at least one approach beyond the AI's menu. That requirement is an exercise in independent thinking, not a claim that every novel idea will be better. It creates a reason to keep investigating after the system has produced an apparently complete answer.

You may ultimately adopt much of the response. The important work is deciding why. Keep the suggestions that survive examination, revise those that need qualification, and discard the ones that do not fit the sources or the client's circumstances.

## Ask for help that makes you work through the question.

An AI tool can be useful before it produces any replacement prose. Ask it to question your reasoning, point you toward a passage worth rereading, or explain why a distinction matters. Then attempt the next step yourself.

```prompt
I have read the supplied materials and written the answer below. Ask me a question about the weakest part of my reasoning, then wait for my response. Point me to the relevant source passage so I can work through the issue myself. Distinguish what the source establishes from an inference you are drawing. Do not write a replacement answer yet.

Permitted materials: [identify them].
My answer: [paste your attempt].
```

This approach is useful when learning a doctrine, but it also fits an experienced lawyer entering an unfamiliar area. You need to know which parts of the explanation you understand and which you are merely repeating. Continue practicing important skills without AI as well, especially when your work or course will require you to perform independently.
