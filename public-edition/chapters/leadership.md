# Decide how the technology should change your work.
> Better tools give lawyers more choices about how to deliver a service. We still need to decide which changes help clients, how people will learn, and who is responsible when something goes wrong.

## Examine what an existing practice is doing for people.

A familiar task may serve more than one purpose. A junior lawyer's research helps answer the client's question, but it also develops the lawyer's ability to evaluate authority. A proposed automation might shorten the research while reducing that opportunity to learn. A responsible redesign needs to account for both effects.

The same question applies to client service. A quicker document may be useful, but a client facing a difficult decision may also need time to ask questions and discuss consequences. Measure the change against the service you want to provide, rather than assuming that less production time answers every question.

We should be willing to reconsider inherited practices. Some protect values that matter; others may persist because no one has examined them closely. Find out what a step contributes before deciding whether to preserve, change, or remove it.

## Return to important questions as your experience develops.

The questions below concern legal work, clients, institutions, and the public. They do not have an answer key. Choose one that you can connect with your experience and write your current view.

Return after trying a technique, completing a matter, or participating in a course or workshop. What did you learn that bears on the question? You do not have to change your mind, but try to give a more precise account of why you hold the view and what evidence might change it.

The three spaces let you keep an initial response and revisit it twice. There is no required timetable, and choosing a different question leaves your earlier answers saved.

## Make it possible to question impressive-looking work.

Someone needs to be able to say that a source is missing, a conclusion is overstated, or a workflow is not ready. That becomes harder when a team treats speed or visible AI use as the main sign of success.

When a colleague identifies a problem, investigate how it arose. Was the assignment unclear? Did the reviewer lack access to the relevant source? Did an early assumption become difficult to question after several later steps relied on it? Those questions help you improve the process instead of treating every failure as an isolated mistake.

Give people a workable way to pause an assignment and obtain a decision. Responsibility without enough time, information, or authority to act on it is difficult to exercise. Leaders should make the review possible, not merely require someone to sign off at the end.

## Look for capabilities you can demonstrate.

Finishing the chapters does not establish that a method is ready for a live matter. Look for evidence in the work. Can you explain the problem before prompting and identify the risks of the proposed use? Can you trace an important conclusion to appropriate support and decide what to do when that support is missing?

For a multi-step assignment, can you specify permissions and review points, then test whether the system follows them? For process improvement, can you show why a change helps the person using the service? For a prototype, can you describe its limits and the tests it still needs?

Leadership and professional responsibility connect those skills. You should be able to explain how the team can question the work, who may approve it, and what AI contributed to the final result. These are capabilities to develop through practice, not titles acquired by using a particular product.

## Carry one useful change into the next assignment.

Look back at an exercise or note that changed how you approached the work. Describe the change in a way a colleague could use. Include the circumstance in which it helped and a limitation that someone reusing it should understand.

Then choose the next thing to investigate. You might need a more specific drafting instruction, a better check for a recurring task, or time with someone whose experience reveals a problem you have overlooked. Keep the proposed step small enough that you can try it and learn from the result.

That is how this guide is intended to be used beyond a course or workshop. Return to the explanations when you need them, adapt the methods to work you understand, and keep examining whether the assistance is making the legal service better.
