# Improve the process before adding more technology.
> Begin with the experience of the person receiving the service. Find out where the work becomes slow, confusing, or unreliable, then decide whether AI would improve it.
@nav Improve a process

## Define the result from the client's perspective.

A team can produce an excellent document and still make the service difficult to use. An intake form may ask for the same information twice. A client may receive a carefully drafted explanation but remain unsure what to send next. A request may sit unanswered because no one knows who is responsible for the follow-up.

Before choosing a tool, describe what should be different when the service works well. For an intake process, that might mean the client understands what information is needed and the reviewing lawyer receives it in a usable form. “Generate a summary” describes one task; it does not establish that broader result.

Include the people who handle the work between the obvious legal steps. An assistant may know why forms come back incomplete. A client may reveal that a question you considered straightforward is hard to answer. Their experience helps you identify what to change before automating the current process.

## Work from the current process toward a testable change.

I use nine questions to organize this work. Begin by **defining the outcome** and **mapping what happens now**. Include who performs each step and what the next person receives. Then **find the sources of delay or failure**: repeated work, missing information, unclear responsibility, or a point where an error can pass unnoticed.

Next, ask what you can **remove or clarify before adding a tool**. A redundant question may need to disappear. An ambiguous request may need better wording. **Assign the work to the right person or system**, and **build the necessary review and restrictions** around the steps where mistakes would matter.

Finally, **test a limited version** with people who resemble the intended users. **Measure the whole result**, including review and correction, rather than only the time to produce a draft. **Revise the process and keep a record of the change** so you can compare versions and repeat the important tests.

These questions give you a way to investigate the service. You may return to the beginning when testing reveals that you misunderstood the client's need. That is useful information about the proposed change, not a reason to hide an unsuccessful test.

## Map a process you understand well enough to examine.

Choose a familiar service and describe it without client or other sensitive information. Start with the current steps, even if you already have a proposed improvement. Write one step per line and identify who handles it. Then use the remaining questions to explain the problem and the change you would test.

## Measure the work that happens after the draft appears.

A faster first draft may shift work to the reviewer. A shorter form may produce more follow-up questions. To judge the proposed process, include those consequences in the comparison.

For a document-summary task, record meaningful errors and the time needed to check and repair them. For intake, consider how often information is missing and where users need help. Where the change affects access, learning, or the distribution of work, examine those effects too. The measures should fit the service you are trying to improve.

For an organization managing several AI uses, NIST's AI Risk Management Framework and its Generative AI Profile provide a broader reference. They address responsibility, context, measurement, and response to risk. The framework is a voluntary resource, not a certification conferred by completing this worksheet. Use the linked source notes to explore it when the organizational question calls for more detail.

## Try the revised process with someone who has not helped design it.

Consider a fictional clinic or firm intake process. Choose one instruction that people are likely to misunderstand. Rewrite it, explain what information or protection the revised version must preserve, and ask someone else to use it without your coaching.

Watch where they hesitate. Do they know what the question is asking? Can they find what happens next? A lawyer might test with a colleague or a representative user; students can begin with classmates using fictional facts. In either setting, avoid using a live client service as an unsupervised experiment.

Compare what happened with what you expected. You may discover that the main problem was the order of the questions rather than their wording, or that the process needs a person available at a particular point. Record that finding before deciding whether an AI feature would help. The test should improve your understanding of the service as well as the proposed tool.
