# Good AI work begins with good lawyering
> AI gives us more ways to work on a legal problem. The useful question is where that assistance will improve the work, and what we need to contribute before anyone relies on it.
@nav Your role as a lawyer

## Find out what the client is trying to accomplish.

A client asks whether it can terminate a software contract. We could give an AI tool the agreement and ask for an answer. But first, we need to understand why the client wants out. Repeated outages might be interrupting its business. It might have found a cheaper alternative. Or it might prefer to keep the vendor if the relationship can be repaired.

Those differences affect the assignment. A careful analysis of termination rights will not answer whether the client can afford to lose the service next week. And a forceful demand letter may undermine a negotiation the client would rather preserve. Before asking AI for help, write down the decision the work should support. You can then decide which legal and factual questions deserve attention.

This is where I want us to begin. We will spend plenty of time improving prompts and working through examples. But the tools become more useful when we bring a clearer understanding of the legal work to them. Throughout this guide, ask whether you could explain the result to the person who needs it, including what remains uncertain and why your recommendation fits their circumstances.

## Your contribution extends beyond producing the answer.

Consider everything that happens around a useful piece of legal advice. You **frame** the problem by separating the client's objective from the first legal question they ask. You **judge** the available options, including their practical consequences. You **counsel** the client through a choice that may remain difficult even after the law is clear.

You also **design** how the work will get done. Someone needs to identify the relevant documents, resolve conflicting accounts, and decide when a draft is ready for review. Along the way, you **challenge** the assumptions behind the emerging answer. Finally, you **own** what you approve: you should be able to explain the recommendation without relying on the fact that a system produced it confidently.

These responsibilities overlap. In the vendor matter, learning that the client cannot tolerate an interruption may send you back to the contract to investigate transition assistance. That return to the sources is valuable legal work. AI can assist with it, but it needs to be directed toward the problem you have identified.

## Supervising AI draws on skills lawyers already use.

You do not need to become a software engineer to start working this way. Think about a well-run assignment to a junior lawyer. You explain the question, supply background they would not otherwise know, and describe what you need back. For a difficult matter, you may ask to discuss the research before they draft. You also make clear which decisions require your involvement.

Much of that discipline carries over to AI. The difference is that you should not assume the system shares a colleague's understanding of the matter or will recognize when an instruction needs clarification. Make important limits explicit, and check what the tool actually did. Later chapters show how to organize those instructions, sources, and reviews into a repeatable process.

These skills matter at every career stage. A student can practice by planning a fictional assignment and explaining where review belongs. An experienced lawyer can apply the same questions to a recurring matter that the team already understands. In both settings, the aim is to make the work easier to direct and evaluate.

## Choose an AI contribution you can judge.

Start with a task you understand well enough to assess. After reading two contract provisions, you might ask AI to identify their differences. After developing an argument, you might ask what a skeptical reader would need explained. Each request gives the system a useful job without handing it every decision in the matter.

Before you send the request, decide what would make the response useful. A comparison should preserve differences that affect meaning and point you to the language behind them. A critique should engage with your reasoning, rather than recite objections that could apply to any argument. This gives you a standard beyond whether the answer sounds polished.

Your initial view may change. That is often the benefit of using another perspective. What matters is that you can explain the change. If the system identifies an exception you missed, return to the source and decide what the exception does to your analysis.

## Apply this to an assignment you know.

Choose a fictional matter or describe a familiar assignment without client or other sensitive information. What does the person receiving the work need to decide? Which part of the assignment would you ask AI to help with? Explain what you would need to examine before using that contribution.
